* Reviewer 2 — Plain-Language Explanations
This section unpacks each of Reviewer 2's demands so the required
action is clear.

** Font uniformization (Abstract and full manuscript)
The reviewer noticed that algorithm names (Logistic Regression,
LightGBM, XGBoost, etc.) are rendered in a different typeface than the
surrounding text — likely because they were pasted from code or another
source and retained a monospace or sans-serif font. The fix is to
select those terms in the LaTeX source and ensure they use the same
font command as the rest of the text (i.e., remove any =\texttt{}=,
=\textsf{}=, or similar wrappers that differ from the body font).

** Software versions and manufacturer city
Standard clinical/medical journal practice requires that every software
tool cited (Python, scikit-learn, SHAP, AWS SageMaker Canvas, etc.) be
referenced with its exact version number and the city where the
developer or vendor is headquartered (e.g., "scikit-learn v1.3,
Paris, France"). This allows readers to reproduce the work and
understand the regulatory/commercial context.

** PE incidence vs. prevalence in the dataset
- *Incidence* here means: out of all pregnancies in the dataset, what
  fraction developed PE? The reviewer wants this number reported
  explicitly (e.g., "X out of Y patients had PE, representing Z%").
- *Implications on performance*: if PE cases are rare (low incidence),
  a model can achieve high accuracy by simply predicting "no PE" for
  everyone — this is known as the accuracy paradox. Conversely, if PE
  cases are over-represented, performance metrics may be inflated.
  Either way, the reviewer wants us to discuss whether the class
  balance (or imbalance) affects the reliability of the reported
  metrics.
- *High prevalence concern*: the reviewer flags that the PE rate in our
  cohort appears unusually high compared to the general population
  (~3–5%). We must explain why (e.g., referral-center bias, inclusion
  criteria, case enrichment) so that readers understand the external
  validity of the model.

** Gestational age at which features were recorded
PE can manifest differently in the first, second, and third trimester.
A model trained on third-trimester variables cannot be used for
early-pregnancy screening. The reviewer wants a clear statement of
*when* (in gestational weeks or trimester) each feature in the dataset
was collected, so the clinical applicability of the model is
unambiguous.

** Single vs. repeated laboratory measurements
For variables like platelets, creatinine, and proteinuria/24 h, the
reviewer asks whether each patient has *one* value (taken at a
specific, defined moment) or *multiple longitudinal values* (repeated
over time). This matters because:
- If a single measurement, we must state exactly when it was taken
  (e.g., at admission, at diagnosis).
- If multiple measurements, including all of them as features can
  introduce data leakage (using post-diagnosis lab values to predict
  diagnosis) and artificially boost performance.

** Inter-group comparisons of all variables
This means adding a table (usually "Table X: Baseline characteristics")
that compares the PE group versus the non-PE group for every feature
used in the models, with appropriate statistical tests (t-test or
Mann–Whitney U for continuous variables; chi-squared for categorical)
and the resulting p-values. Without this, the reader cannot judge
whether the variables actually differ between groups, making the
model's predictive power opaque.

** Non-PE-specific variables (SpO2, chest pain)
SpO2 (oxygen saturation) and chest pain can be low/present in many
conditions (pulmonary embolism, cardiac disease, anaemia, etc.) and are
not part of the standard diagnostic criteria for PE. Including them
risks training a model that is actually detecting general illness
severity rather than PE specifically. The reviewer asks us to either
remove them or provide a strong clinical justification backed by PE
guidelines for keeping them.

** Chronic hypertension exclusion
Chronic (pre-existing) hypertension and PE share overlapping features
(high blood pressure, proteinuria) but have different causes and
treatment implications. Including chronic hypertension patients can
make PE diagnosis look artificially easy because those patients already
have elevated BP readings. The reviewer suggests excluding them (or
analyzing them separately) to avoid confounding the PE signal.

** Perfect Logistic Regression performance / overfitting
A Logistic Regression model achieving 100% (or near-100%) on any
metric is almost always a sign of *data leakage* or *overfitting*:
- *Leakage*: a feature that is recorded *as a result of* PE diagnosis
  (e.g., a treatment variable, a lab value taken after diagnosis) was
  included as a predictor. The model learns the outcome, not the
  clinical signal.
- *Overfitting to the feature set*: too many correlated or
  post-diagnostic variables make a simple linear model perfectly
  separate the classes on the training/test data, but the model would
  fail on truly prospective data.
  We must audit the feature list against PE diagnosis timelines and
  guidelines, and report learning curves or cross-validation results
  that demonstrate generalizability.

** PE sub-categories: early vs. late, moderate vs. severe
The clinical management of PE differs substantially by subtype. A model
that predicts "PE vs. no PE" is less actionable than one that also
identifies high-risk subtypes. The reviewer asks us to stratify
analyses by:
- *Early-onset PE* (< 34 weeks) vs. *late-onset PE* ($\geq$ 34 weeks) — they
  have different pathophysiology and outcomes.
- *Moderate PE* vs. *severe PE* (or severe features) — severity guides
  treatment urgency.
If our dataset is too small for sub-group modeling, we should
acknowledge this as a limitation.

** Discussion: comparison with similar studies
The reviewer warns against cherry-picking comparison studies. We should
only compare our results with papers that used (a) similar input
variables, (b) similar preprocessing and modeling approaches, and (c)
similar patient populations. Comparing with studies that used different
variable sets or different PE definitions would be misleading. "Less is
more" here means: a few well-chosen, directly comparable references are
better than a long list of loosely related ones.

** Strengths and limitations paragraph
Every clinical ML paper is expected to explicitly list what the study
does well (e.g., real-world hospital data, use of explainability via
SHAP, comparison with commercial AutoML) and what it cannot claim
(e.g., single center, retrospective design, synthetic data augmentation,
small sample size, no external validation cohort). This paragraph is
standard practice and helps editors and readers calibrate the
contribution.
