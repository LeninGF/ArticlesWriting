The presence of the pectoral muscle in Medio Lateral Oblique (MLO) mammograms presents a significant challenge for Computer-Aided Detection (CAD) systems due to its intensity and textural similarity to glandular tissue. This study proposes a hybrid segmentation pipeline applied to the MIAS database that integrates traditional image processing with modern foundation models to segment both the pectoral muscle and breast anomalies. The methodology employs a semi-automatic approach for pectoral muscle removal using manual polygon selection and morphological operations, alongside a recursive Otsu thresholding algorithm for the segmentation of masses. To address the scarcity of expert radiologist annotations, the study utilizes the Segment Anything Model (SAM) to generate simulated ground truth masks for quantitative validation. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that the proposed method achieves high volumetric agreement with a mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 0.9383. However, an Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD) of 6.57 mm indicates moderate discrepancies in boundary delineation. These findings suggest that while SAM-derived masks serve as a robust proxy for benchmarking in resource-constrained environments, further fine-tuning is required to achieve the precision necessary for clinical contouring.

new cases and approximately \numprint{670000} deaths reported in the

global burden of the disease and reinforce the importance of early

Early detection and diagnose of \ac{BC} is crucial to reduce the
number of deaths caused by the disease. Due to this, regular \ac{BC}
screening is highly recommended by healthcare professionals. Digital
\ac{MG} is currently the most effective imaging method for detecting
and diagnosing breast cancer at an early stage. Common signs of
\ac{BC} on mammograms include calcifications, masses or lumps,
asymmetries, and changes in the normal structure of the

\section{Medical Image Segmentation}\label{sec:Medical Image Segmentation}

Medical image segmentation plays a pivotal role in clinical diagnostics and treatment planning, enabling precise delineation of anatomical structures and pathological regions~\cite{Berrezueta2025}. The field has undergone significant evolution, transitioning from traditional methods to deep learning-based approaches, and more recently, to foundation models capable of generalizing across diverse tasks and modalities.

\subsection{Traditional and Deep Learning-Based Methods}

Early segmentation techniques relied on manual feature extraction or handcrafted algorithms such as thresholding, edge detection, and morphological operations~\cite{Yao2023}. While interpretable, these methods often struggled with the complexity and variability of medical images. The advent of deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), marked a paradigm shift. Architectures such as U-Net~\cite{RonnebergerFB15} and its variants (e.g., UNet++~\cite{zhou2018unetplusplus}) became the state-of-the-art (SOTA) for medical image segmentation due to their ability to learn hierarchical features and preserve spatial context through encoder-decoder structures with skip connections.

Despite their success, CNN-based models are limited in capturing long-range dependencies due to the inductive biases of convolutional operations. This limitation spurred the integration of Transformer architectures~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, which leverage self-attention mechanisms to model global context. Models like TransUNet~\cite{chen2021transunet} and Swin-Unet~\cite{cao2022swinunet} combined the local feature extraction of CNNs with the global attention mechanism of Transformers, achieving SOTA performance on various medical segmentation tasks~\cite{yao2023review}.

\subsection{Foundational Models for Image Segmentation}

The introduction of the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023segment} represented a breakthrough in natural image segmentation, offering remarkable zero-shot generalization capabilities through prompt-based segmentation. However, its direct application to medical images proved suboptimal due to fundamental differences between natural and medical imaging domains, such as low contrast, ambiguous boundaries, and modality-specific artifacts~\cite{Ma2024,Berrezueta2025}.

To address these challenges, several medical adaptations of SAM were developed. MedSAM~\cite{Ma2024} was among the first foundation models tailored for medical imaging, fine-tuned on a large-scale dataset comprising over 1.57 million image-mask pairs across 10 modalities and 30 cancer types. It demonstrated superior performance compared to both vanilla SAM and modality-specific specialist models, highlighting the potential of large-scale domain-specific fine-tuning.

Further innovations followed, such as MedSAM-2~\cite{zhu2024medsam2}, which extended SAM’s capabilities to 3D medical images by treating volumetric data as video sequences and introducing a \emph{self-sorting memory bank} to handle unordered slices. This approach enabled \emph{one-prompt segmentation}, allowing the model to generalize from a single prompt to segment similar structures across multiple images without temporal relationships. Such advances significantly reduce the need for extensive user interaction, making these models more practical for clinical deployment.

\section{Related Works}\label{sec:RelatedWorks}

Research on digital mammography over the last decade has revealed a clear progression from rule-based segmentation approaches to more sophisticated optimization and deep learning methods.  Early work, such as that of Bora \emph{et, al.}\cite{Bora2016}, relied on texture gradients and geometric models to approximate the pectoral boundary and achieved respectable accuracy on small datasets.  Similarly, Shah\cite{Shah2015} applied morphological filtering, wavelet enhancement, and a triangular mask to suppress the muscle region, reporting high accuracy on mini-MIAS images.  These methods are straightforward and computationally efficient, but they rely on heuristic assumptions about muscle shape and offer limited evaluation beyond basic error metrics.

Subsequent studies introduced optimization techniques to improve robustness. Shen \emph{et al.}\cite{Shen2018} employed a genetic algorithm combined with morphological selection to automatically learn multilevel thresholds. The test was carried out on the miniMIAS, DDSM and INBreast datasets, and achieved low false positive and false negative rates with Dice scores of up to 97.15\%. However, the translation of these quantitative improvements into better clinical outcomes was not explored. Chen \emph{et al.}\cite{Chen2024} proposed a faster pipeline using binarization, Canny edge detection, and robust interpolation. Their method reduced the mean segmentation error compared to the Libra algorithm and processed images 5.4 times faster, though false negatives remained relatively high. The clinical impact of these efficiency gains was not evaluated. An intermediate study by Sharma and Mukherjee proposed a comprehensive segmentation pipeline that combines filtering, multilevel thresholding, and edge detection; however, it did not report quantitative metrics~\cite{Sharma2021}. These works demonstrate that algorithmic refinement can yield incremental improvements, but discussions on direct clinical outcomes remain limited.

A 2024 review by Al-Karawi \emph{et. al.}\cite{AlKarawi2024} summarizes these developments, noting that modern approaches like Mask--RCNN combined with graph convolutional networks reach accuracies near 99\% in benchmark datasets.  The review emphasizes the trend toward hybrid architectures that integrate classical image processing with deep networks, but also highlights that reported performances stem from heterogeneous evaluations, which limit direct comparison.
The most significant leap comes from deep learning.  Yu \emph{et, al.}\cite{Yu2022} integrated a global channel attention module into Deeplabv3+, producing PeMNet, which achieved an intersection--over--union of 97.46\% and pixel accuracy of 99.48\% on combined OPTIMAM and INBreast data.  This architecture leverages feature hierarchies and attention mechanisms to outperform classical methods.  In a related but distinct direction, TorabiJafroudi \emph{et, al.}\cite{Torabi2024} used the Curvelet transform and a genetic algorithm to select features for classifying masses as benign or malignant.  Their six-fold cross-validation achieved 98.2\% accuracy and 100\% specificity, illustrating the potential of multiresolution analysis coupled with machine learning.

Across these studies, a recurring theme is the reliance on quantitative error metrics (e.g., false positives/negatives, Dice, Jaccard, IoU) as proxies for performance.  While useful, such metrics do not fully capture generalisability, computational cost, or clinical relevance.  Most datasets remain modest in size or confined to specific repositories, and external validation is seldom performed.  Consequently, despite impressive numerical results, the broader impact on diagnostic workflows is unclear.  Future research would benefit from larger, more diverse cohorts, standardized evaluation protocols, and assessments of how improvements in segmentation or classification translate to enhanced patient outcomes.

\subsubsection{Filtering and Enhancing}

Medical images are often degraded by noise arising from interference or other phenomena that affect measurement processes in imaging acquisition systems. The presence of noise is intrinsic to these systems and often constitutes a limiting factor in the performance of medical instruments. For example, recent work on two-stage deep denoising with self-guided noise attention demonstrates substantial improvements in PSNR and SSIM for multimodal medical images using residual noise estimation. ~\cite{Sharif_2024} In addition, the physiological systems under study and the procedures used in radiology influence both image contrast and the visibility of structural details; techniques such as PSO-ERBMAHE for adaptive contrast enhancement have been shown to better preserve visibility of anatomical features, especially in low-contrast regions. ~\cite{Gangwar2025} Consequently, it is often necessary to adjust the grayscale intensity range to enhance the visualization of brighter regions, which are less distinguishable to the human eye compared to darker areas that are perceived with greater detail.

%In this work, a Laplacian filter is applied during the preprocessing stage to enhance edge detail and improve contrast. The Laplacian filter is a second-order derivative filter used in image processing to highlight areas with rapid intensity changes, thereby sharpening edges and enhancing fine details. In mammography, it is applied to improve the visibility of subtle features, such as microcalcifications and masses, which are critical for early breast cancer detection. Often combined with Gaussian smoothing to form the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filter, it reduces noise while enhancing edges. Scientific studies have demonstrated its effectiveness, including its use in normalizing mammographic images for cancer segmentation (Rangayyan, 2000), a modified parallel framework for sharpening mammograms and identifying masses using GPU (Wong et al., 2011), and multiscale approaches based on Laplacian pyramids for feature enhancement (Burt and Adelson, 1983).

%Other approaches similar to Khan et al. (2023) \cite{khan2023fast} with the Fast Local Laplacian filter for angiography images, and Shukla et al. These techniques demonstrate that Laplacian-based filters can help preserve important structural details while reducing the impact of low-contrast regions.
Traditional image enhancement methods have relied primarily on spatial- and frequency-domain processing. Spatial approaches operate directly on image pixels, such as histogram equalization or unsharp masking, while frequency domain methods utilize mathematical transforms such as Fourier, DCT, or DWT to process the image in an alternative space. With technological advancements, more sophisticated techniques have emerged, including the Retinex model, fuzzy theory, and deep neural networks. Although each method provides distinct advantages, they also exhibit limitations, particularly in robustness and adaptability. This highlights the challenge of developing a universally effective enhancement technique suitable for various image types and conditions.~\cite{Qi2022}


In our work, the enhancement and artifact removal process consists of several fundamental steps. Initially, automatic threshold-based binarization is applied using the \texttt{graythresh} and \texttt{im2bw} function, or \texttt{imbinarize} in more recent versions of Matlab-2024b.\cite{matlab_imbinarize} These functions convert the image to binary format by selecting a threshold that minimizes the intraclass variance between the foreground (white) and background (black) pixels Fig.~\ref{fig2}.
Following binarization, a series of morphological operations are performed to extract the relevant image components. These operations enable the representation and description of region shapes, including boundary extraction, peak and hole removal, object labeling, area and bounding box computation, selection of the largest region, and identification of the ROI. More procedures are then applied to refine the boundary delineation and remove residual artifacts.

\subsubsection{Contrast Enhancement}

To improve image quality, we used an unsharp masking technique, which enhances high-frequency content, thereby improving edge definition and fine detail visibility. Unsharp masking is commonly used in medical image pre-processing to enhance sharpness and emphasize. Furthermore, the appropriate choice of these techniques is greatly influenced by the imaging modality, the task at hand, and the viewing conditions.~\cite{maini2010comprehensivereviewimageenhancement}
In our approach, a $3 \times 3$ unsharp mask was applied. The specific mask used is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig3} and was previously validated in our previous work on the MIAS image dataset, where it demonstrated strong performance~\cite{7314222}.

\subsubsection{Recursive Otsu threshold segmentation}
The objective of this method is to segment an image into multiple intensity classes using Otsu recursively: first, a global threshold is obtained; then, a local threshold is calculated on the lower subset and another on the upper subset, generating binary masks covering four ranges (plus the global mask).
The Otsu thresholding method was originally proposed to segment images using a single threshold that maximizes the variance between-classes~\cite{4310076} ~\cite{bioengineering11101034} However, many applications require partitioning the image into more than two intensity regions, leading to the development of multilevel extensions.~\cite{bioengineering11101034} Among these, hierarchical or recursive approaches apply the Otsu criterion iteratively to pixel subsets to obtain adaptive multiple thresholds. This procedure, known as recursive Otsu thresholding, has been successfully used in domains such as medical image analysis and remote sensing because of its simplicity and effectiveness when dealing with multimodal histograms. \subsection*{Definitions and brief descriptions}
	In binary segmentation, the Dice coefficient and the F\textsubscript{1}-score are algebraically equivalent. To quantitatively evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we use the following metrics: FP rate, FN rate. ~\cite{Shen2018}

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC): The Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) is a measure of overlap between predicted segmentation masks and ground truth masks, commonly used for evaluating segmentation models ~\cite{diagnostics14080848}(Eq.2). DSC measures the ratio of the correctly predicted positive pixels over the number of positive areas in both the ground truth and the prediction mask, considering the false positive rate in the calculations alongside false negatives.  
        \item Jaccard, overlap ratio between prediction and ground truth; stricter than Dice. ~\cite{4781564}. (Eq.3)
        \item Sensitivity: Measures the false negative rate (Eq. 4), complementing the assessment by analyzing the detection of relevant areas.
        \item Accuracy: Although used (Eq. 7), it is not sufficient alone, as the pectoral muscles occupy a small portion of the images, limiting its informative capacity. ~\cite{Shen2018}
    \end{itemize}

	\begin{description}
		\item[Precision:]
		\[
		\mathrm{Prec} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}       	\tag {1}                
		\]
		%\textit{Proportion of predicted positives that are correct; reflects false positive control.}
		
		\item[Dice Coefficient (F\textsubscript{1} for sets):]
		\[
		\mathrm{Dice} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}
        	\tag{2}
		\]
		%\textit{Harmonic overlap; equals the F\textsubscript{1}-score in binary segmentation.}

        \item[Jaccard Index (IoU):]
		\[
		\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}
        	\tag{3}
		\]
		%\textit{Overlap ratio between prediction and ground truth; stricter than Dice.} ~\cite{4781564}
		
        
        \item[Recall (Sensitivity):]

        	\tag{4}
		\]
		%\textit{Proportion of actual positives that are recovered; reflects false negative control.}

		%\textit{Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall; balances both error types.}

    In the context of automated generation of ground truth datasets for mammography image segmentation, evaluating the quality of simulated annotations is critical to ensure their reliability for training and validating machine learning models. A key metric for this assessment is the \textbf{Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD)}, which quantifies the spatial accuracy between the contours of predicted and reference masks. This section outlines the criteria for interpreting a simulated ground truth as ``high quality'' using ASSD, complemented by metrics such as the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and Intersection over Union (IoU), while considering factors specific to mammography imaging.~\cite{Yeghiazaryan2018}

\subsection{Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD)}
The ASSD measures the average distance between corresponding points on the contours of the predicted and reference (ground truth) masks, providing a robust indicator of segmentation accuracy, particularly for boundary delineation.~\cite{Yeghiazaryan2018} ~\cite{4781564} This metric is especially relevant in mammography, where precise contouring of structures such as lesions or breast tissue is essential for clinical applications. The ideal ASSD values for simulated ground truth are interpreted as follows~\cite{Shen2018}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ASSD Ideal ($< 1$ mm)}: An ASSD below 1 mm indicates excellent segmentation, with near-perfect alignment between the simulated and reference contours. This level of precision is comparable to expert manual annotations and is highly desirable for mammography datasets used in diagnostic model training.
    \item \textbf{ASSD Good (1 mm to 5 mm)}: Values in this range are considered acceptable for most clinical applications in mammography. Minor discrepancies in this range typically do not compromise diagnostic accuracy, reflecting robust performance of the ground truth generation algorithm.
    \item \textbf{ASSD Reasonable (5 mm to 10 mm)}: ASSD values between 5 mm and 10 mm may be tolerable in exploratory settings or for structures with inherent variability (e.g., irregular lesions). However, such values suggest a moderate margin of error, indicating potential for refinement in the simulation process.
    \item \textbf{ASSD Poor ($> 10$ mm)}: ASSD values exceeding 10 mm signify significant contour misalignment, rendering the simulated ground truth unreliable for clinical applications. Such datasets require substantial improvements to ensure validity.
\end{itemize}

These thresholds are derived from standard practices in medical image segmentation, adjusted for the challenges of simulated ground truth generation in mammography.
In addition, combining ASSD with DSC and IoU provides a multifaceted assessment, where ASSD emphasizes boundary precision, and DSC/IoU evaluate volumetric consistency, both critical for mammography segmentation.

\section{Discussion}
Although the simulated ground truth generated with SAM provides accurate binary masks that approximate expert-level annotations, it cannot fully substitute for the radiologist-delineated ground truth. Expert annotations remain the gold standard for clinical validation, as they incorporate contextual knowledge and interpretative criteria beyond the pixel-level delineation. However, the simulated ground truth offers a practical and reproducible solution for algorithm development and preliminary validation, enabling reliable benchmarking in the absence of expert annotations.

The high Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC = 0.9383) indicates that the masks generated by SAM closely match the expert manual segmentation in terms of overall area coverage. However, the Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD = 6.57 mm) reveals some misalignment along the contours. This finding aligns with the fact that SAM has not been specifically trained for refining anatomical boundaries.
Despite these boundary discrepancies, the results suggest that SAM can provide a strong baseline or proxy for ground-truth annotations in situations where manual labeling is time-consuming or impractical. Nonetheless, manual adjustments may still be required in applications that demand highly precise boundary delineation.

\section{Limitations of the Study}
One important limitation of this study is that the ground truth was generated using SAM-based segmentation instead of expert radiologist annotations. While the simulated ground truth ensures accuracy and reproducibility for benchmarking, it lacks the clinical insight and interpretative depth of manual delineations. Future work will incorporate radiologist-annotated datasets to enhance the clinical validity of the evaluation and to verify the robustness of the proposed method under real-world conditions.

Lack of Fine-Tuning: In zero-shot mode, fine-tuning SAM could improve accuracy.
Prompt Dependence: The quality of SAM masks depends on the prompts provided (e.g., dots or boxes in the pectoral muscle). If the prompts were inconsistent or inaccurate, this could contribute to a high ASSD.
Limited Validation: Using SAM as ground truth assumes that its masks are a suitable reference standard. However, since SAM is not specifically trained for mammography, its validity as ground truth must be carefully evaluated against human annotations.

Despite these limitations, the findings highlight the potential of the proposed approach, providing a solid basis for future validation with expert-annotated datasets and paving the way toward clinically relevant applications



\section{Conclusions and Future Works}\label{sec:Conclusions}

This work proposes the integration of modern and traditional techniques to achieve robust segmentation of the pectoral muscle and the abnormalities present in MIAS mammographic images.

In our study, we employed the Segment Anything Model (SAM) to generate segmentation masks for mammography images, evaluated against manual segmentation using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and Average Symmetric Surface Distance (ASSD). The obtained DSC of 0.9383 indicates excellent overlap between the SAM-generated masks and manual segmentations, reflecting high fidelity in capturing the overall region of interest. However, the ASSD of 6.57 mm suggests a reasonable but suboptimal alignment of contours, indicating moderate boundary discrepancies. While the DSC confirms robust volumetric agreement, the ASSD value, falling within the 5–10 mm range, highlights the need for further refinement to achieve the precision required for clinical applications in mammography, where accurate contour delineation is critical.

These results demonstrate that the SAM-based simulated ground truth can serve as a valid reference for quantitative evaluation, ensuring robust validation of the proposed approach.

To improve SAM’s performance in pectoral muscle segmentation for mammography, future work should focus on enhancing contour precision (ASSD) by fine-tuning with diverse datasets and applying boundary refinement methods, such as conditional random fields or active contours. Incorporating metrics like Hausdorff Distance and validating on multi-center datasets will ensure robustness. Integrating SAM with specialized models or testing in clinical workflows will align performance with diagnostic needs, targeting ASSD $< 5$ mm.
